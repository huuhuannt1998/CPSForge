\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We situate CPSForge at the intersection of five active research areas: LLM-driven attack generation for industrial systems, autonomous cyber agents, PLC-based CPS security testbeds, runtime safety enforcement, and adaptive adversarial defense. Table~\ref{tab:comparison} summarizes the positioning.

\subsection{LLM-Based Attack Generation for CPS}

The application of large language models to cyber-physical attack planning is nascent. Ahmed~\cite{ahmed2025attackllm} introduced \emph{AttackLLM}, using GPT-4 to generate attack patterns for the SWaT water-treatment testbed. While effective at producing semantically plausible attack descriptions offline, AttackLLM does not embed the LLM inside a live execution loop, lacks a runtime safety shield, includes no defender, and does not support closed-loop adaptation. Gowdanakatte et al.~\cite{gowdanakatte2025threat} proposed an automated threat-emulation framework that uses LLMs to plan CPS attack sequences, but evaluation remains at the threat-intelligence planning level rather than live PLC-level execution. Ning et al.~\cite{ning2025malf} presented MALF, a multi-agent LLM framework for fuzzing industrial control protocols at the network layer; however, it operates on Modbus/S7Comm packets rather than process-level tag manipulation and does not include a defender or shield. Garcia et al.~\cite{garcia2017harvey} introduced HARVEY, a physics-model-aware rootkit that performs man-in-the-middle manipulation of PLC I/O signals by embedding a learned dynamical model of the physical plant. HARVEY established the viability of the PLC-level MitM threat model that CPSForge adopts; however, its attack logic requires a pre-trained plant-specific physics model rather than general-purpose language model reasoning, and it includes no defender or safety shield. CPSForge advances this line by replacing hand-crafted physics models with general-purpose LLM reasoning inside a real PLC execution loop, mediating every write through a configurable safety shield, and pairing the attacker with an autonomous LLM defender.

\subsection{Autonomous Cyber Agents and Red Teaming}

Autonomous penetration-testing agents represent a growing area. Deng et al.~\cite{deng2024pentestgpt} introduced PentestGPT, demonstrating that LLMs can guide multi-step penetration tests on IT targets, and Shen et al.~\cite{shen2025pentestagent} extended this paradigm with PentestAgent. Rigaki et al.~\cite{rigaki2024hackphyr} fine-tuned an LLM agent (Hackphyr) specifically for network security environments. Abuadbba et al.~\cite{abuadbba2025promise} survey the promises and perils of LLM-based red-blue teaming for cybersecurity. The survey by Xu et al.~\cite{xu2025forewarned} catalogues LLM-based agents for autonomous cyberattacks and identifies a significant gap in \emph{operational technology} (OT) and CPS-specific agents. All of the above target IT infrastructure---networks, web applications, and general-purpose operating systems---rather than the real-time, physics-constrained environments of industrial control systems. CPSForge fills this gap by instantiating LLM-driven attacker and defender agents that operate on physical process tags within a real Siemens S7 PLC loop.

\subsection{PLC-Based CPS Security Testbeds}

Testbeds that include real controllers provide the realism needed for credible CPS security evaluation. Mathur and Tippenhauer~\cite{mathur2016swat} established the SWaT testbed and dataset~\cite{goh2017swat}, which remain dominant ICS anomaly-detection benchmarks. Dehlaghi-Ghadim et al.~\cite{dehlaghi2023icssim} propose ICSSIM, and Garcia et al.~\cite{garcia2025sphere} describe the SPHERE CPS Enclave. More recent efforts include SIMPLE-ICS~\cite{pramadi2026simpleics}, plus domain-specific HIL platforms for nuclear~\cite{chen2024nuclear_hil, nam2025hintsec} and thermal~\cite{mathew2024hil_thermal} power plants. Factory~I/O has also been used for Modbus/TCP penetration testing~\cite{lazaridis2023factoryio_pentest} and digital-twin safety analysis~\cite{wang2024factoryio_dt}. None of these testbeds integrate LLM-driven attack or defense agents. CPSForge builds on this tradition with a real Siemens S7-1200 PLC, TIA Portal, and Factory~I/O as a software-based process emulator, adding structured LLM attack planning, autonomous multi-agent operation, and systematic adaptation.

\subsection{Runtime Safety Enforcement for Industrial Systems}

Ensuring that experimental attacks cannot cause irreversible physical harm is critical for any CPS security testbed. Lanotte et al.~\cite{lanotte2022runtime_ics} formalize runtime enforcement for ICS as an edit automaton that rewrites dangerous controller outputs in real time. Baird et al.~\cite{baird2024scalable_enforcement} extend this with a scalable, hardware-amenable enforcement architecture, and Panda et al.~\cite{panda2023incremental_enforcement} propose incremental enforcement to reduce overhead. Alsabbagh and Langend{\"o}rfer~\cite{alsabbagh2023plc_security} survey PLC security challenges and highlight the need for defense layers between the controller and the physical process. CPSForge implements these principles in a practical, configurable \emph{safety shield} that enforces tag whitelists, range bounds, duration caps, cooldown periods, forbidden-combination rules, and scene-specific physical invariants. Unlike prior enforcement work, our shield is designed specifically to mediate LLM-generated actions in a research setting.

\subsection{Adaptive and Adversarial CPS Defense}

Hu et al.~\cite{hu2025evodefender} introduced Evo-Defender, which co-evolves attacker and defender strategies through iterative fuzzing on CPS simulators, demonstrating measurable defender improvement over rounds. Banik et al.~\cite{banik2023adversary_loop} formalize adversary-in-the-loop planning for CPS defense, and Sheikh et al.~\cite{sheikh2023defending_defender} study adversarial robustness of ML-based security methods in CPS. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2023evasion_survey} survey evasion attacks on ML-based CPS detectors, highlighting the arms-race dynamic. Moriano et al.~\cite{moriano2025adaptive_anomaly} provide a systematic review of adaptive anomaly detection for CPS attacks. On the LLM side, Sharshar et al.~\cite{sharshar2025explainable} use LLMs to explain detection events in automatic generation control, and Ann et al.~\cite{ann2024ids_llm} investigate LLM-based intrusion detection for ICS. Santos et al.~\cite{santos2025autopgt} employ LLMs for automated policy generation in ICS. LLM4PLC~\cite{fakih2024llm4plc} and Agents4PLC~\cite{liu2026agents4plc} apply LLMs to PLC code generation and verification, establishing that language models can reason about ladder logic and structured text. CPSForge combines these threads: an LLM attacker generates structured attacks, an LLM defender interprets process telemetry and proposes corrective actions, and a hard-case bank accumulates missed or late detections for iterative defender improvement---all grounded in real PLC execution.

\subsection{Positioning}

Table~\ref{tab:comparison} compares CPSForge to the most related systems. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work combines all of: (i) LLM-driven attack generation over physical process tags, (ii) an autonomous LLM defender operating in the same live loop, (iii) a configurable runtime safety shield mediating every write, (iv) closed-loop adaptation from hard cases, and (v) execution on a real PLC with software-emulated plant simulation. The survey by Xu et al.~\cite{xu2024llm_cybersec_survey} confirms the absence of such integrated frameworks in the current literature.
